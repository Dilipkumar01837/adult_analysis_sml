1. Is the raw gap just chance?

Permutation test: shuffle the "male/female" labels thousands of times and see how big a gap pure luck can create.

How to read this

The bars in the chart are the gaps produced when labels are shuffled randomly (so there is no real link between gender and income). The vertical line is the real gap. If the line sits far outside the shuffled gaps, chance is a poor explanation. The p-value is the share of shuffles that were at least as extreme as the real gap.

The second chart shuffles gender only inside groups of people with the same education level and occupation. Its centre is not zero, because men and women are spread differently across those groups; that centre is the gap you would expect from that mix alone.

2. How much remains after comparing like with like?

Odds ratio (OR) for women vs men. OR = 1 means no difference; below 1 means lower odds for women.

How to read this

Each row adds more controls: age and weekly hours, then years of education, then occupation. If the OR moves toward 1, those factors explain part of the raw gap. If it stays below 1, a gap remains that those variables do not explain. That is not proof of discrimination: other factors may be missing, and education or occupation can themselves be shaped by gender. Odds are not the same as probabilities, but for rare outcomes they behave similarly.

3. Does the gap depend on education?

Gender × education interaction.

How to read this

The likelihood-ratio test asks whether letting the gender gap differ by education fits the data significantly better than a single common gap. A small p-value means the gap really does vary with education. The chart shows the adjusted women-vs-men OR within each education group.
